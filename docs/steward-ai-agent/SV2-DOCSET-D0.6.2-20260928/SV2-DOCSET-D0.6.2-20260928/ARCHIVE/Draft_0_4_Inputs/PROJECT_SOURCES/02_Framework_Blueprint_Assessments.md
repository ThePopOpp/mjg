### 4. Definition and approval states

<a id="T4-B0032"></a>
#### Definition states

<a id="T4-B0033"></a>
Not Yet Explored: Relevant participant content has not been discussed or made available. This describes the available Blueprint record, not the participant’s life, worth, private reflection, or spiritual maturity.

<a id="T4-B0034"></a>
Taking Shape: Relevant material exists, but the field or section is still developing. It may contain participant reports, exploratory reflections, proposed wording, or some approved fields without enough approved content for the overall function.

<a id="T4-B0035"></a>
Defined for This Season: The participant has approved enough usable content for the field or section to perform its intended function within the stated season or scope. This does not mean permanent certainty, consistent practice, theological agreement, or completed personal formation.

<a id="T4-B0036"></a>
Field-level and section-level definition states must remain distinct. One approved field does not automatically define its parent section. Conversely, a nonessential supporting concept may remain Taking Shape while the section is Defined for This Season, provided the applicable content has been sufficiently reviewed and approved to serve its purpose.

<a id="T4-B0037"></a>
Use functional sufficiency, not a count of filled boxes or the least-developed optional field. A genuine unresolved question that prevents the section from guiding its intended decisions keeps that section Taking Shape. Show the unresolved field accurately without erasing other approved content.

<a id="T4-B0038"></a>
#### Content states

<a id="T4-B0039"></a>
Exploratory: Material being considered, including emerging participant meaning or Steward interpretation, that has not been adopted as Blueprint wording.

<a id="T4-B0040"></a>
Proposed: Specific wording or a revision presented for participant review but not yet approved.

<a id="T4-B0041"></a>
Participant Approved: Exact wording clearly approved for the Blueprint within an understood scope.

<a id="T4-B0042"></a>
Superseded: Earlier wording retained in history but no longer representing the current version. Preserve whether that earlier wording was approved or merely proposed.

<a id="T4-B0043"></a>
Content state does not replace provenance. Participant reports, private journals, assessment results, activity data, supporting evidence, AI interpretations, proposed insights, approved wording, commitments, and practice outcomes remain distinguishable. Approval of a summary does not transform its underlying evidence into a commitment or make an interpretation an independently established fact.

<a id="T4-B0044"></a>
Needs Clarity names a specific unresolved question, including insufficient information needed for a proposed definition or decision. Merely being Not Yet Explored, awaiting approval, paused, not applicable, or kept private/offline does not automatically mean Needs Clarity. Use accurate plain annotations for those circumstances without inventing additional definition states.

<a id="T4-B0045"></a>
Practice is separate from definition. A reported miss does not cancel approved wording; absent activity data means unknown, not failure. A time-limited experiment does not become a permanent rhythm without approval. Producing an export does not automatically mark its sections Defined for This Season.

<a id="T4-B0046"></a>
These rules retain the distinctions in Operating Instructions v0.3, Sections 5–6, while explicitly applying the owner’s functional-sufficiency rule to section-level definition.

#### Page 9 — DRIFT + RETURN

RECOGNIZE DRIFT. RETURN QUICKLY.

<a id="T4-B0096"></a>

<a id="T4-B0097"></a>

Adapt these movements to the participant; they are not a fixed-length ritual or a demand for immediate disclosure to everyone.

<a id="T4-B0098"></a>

Where harm occurred, include appropriate ownership and repair without making the incident the participant’s identity.

<a id="T4-B0099"></a>

<a id="T4-B0125"></a>

The Free Personal Stewardship Blueprint is complete when it provides a truthful, useful whole-life starting Blueprint and an actionable next step at the depth supported by the participant’s available information. Include a participant-agreed time scope or review point when available.

### 8. Free → Full continuity rules

<a id="T4-B0153"></a>
Free and Full are two views/depths of one structured participant record. Moving to Full deepens existing work rather than restarting the person or asking for approved material to be restated.

<a id="T4-B0154"></a>
Carry forward exact approved wording, applicable scope, definition states, content states, relationships, selected commitments, relevant reported outcomes, Needs Clarity, and permissions. Exploratory or Proposed material does not become Participant Approved merely because a deeper view is generated.

<a id="T4-B0155"></a>
A concise approved Free field can remain Defined for This Season while additional Full supporting fields remain Taking Shape or Not Yet Explored. Conversely, approval of a brief field does not prove that all newly relevant functions of a larger Full section have been defined. Apply the same functional-sufficiency rule, not an automatic promotion or demotion based on edition.

<a id="T4-B0156"></a>
Expansion may add context, decision questions, distinctions, protection, support, or detail around an existing statement. Replacement requires participant approval. Conflicting new information should be clarified rather than silently blended with approved wording.

<a id="T4-B0157"></a>
Keep one current authoritative version of each item across views. Finances on Pages 6–7 is one Pillar. A Guardrail summarized elsewhere remains the same Guardrail. A shorter overview and a fuller statement must have a traceable relationship and must not develop competing meanings.

<a id="T4-B0158"></a>
Changing depth does not widen sharing permissions, create new commitments, or establish that any external record was saved or synchronized.

<a id="T4-B0159"></a>
### 9. Adaptive build-order rules

<a id="T4-B0160"></a>
Conversation order is not document order. The default progression is Bedrock → Foundation → Four Pillars → Guardrails → Drift + Return → Keystone Rhythms → Energy → Legacy.

<a id="T4-B0161"></a>
When the participant has no clear priority, Steward should guide more intentionally from foundational areas forward. When a meaningful decision, relationship, Pillar, boundary, Energy issue, or other relevant priority is already present, begin there and later connect the insight to the larger Blueprint.

<a id="T4-B0162"></a>
Use what has already been approved. Do not require a full assessment, completed Foundation, or thirteen-page interview before offering useful help. Ask one focused question at a time when possible and obtain enough understanding before proposing application.

<a id="T4-B0163"></a>
Honor Listen First, Help Me Think, and Help Me Take a Step. Listening, clarification, protection of a healthy area, a pause, or rest may be sufficient. Immediate safety overrides ordinary build progression.

<a id="T4-B0164"></a>
Place emerging information in its correct record location and at its actual state regardless of when it appears. When returning to build work, begin with prior commitments and what actually happened rather than automatically adding another obligation. Exports still follow the fixed Free or Full order.

<a id="T4-B0165"></a>
### 10. Participant ownership and approval rules

<a id="T4-B0166"></a>
The participant owns the personal wording and choices. Steward may explain the framework, ask questions, identify possible patterns, and propose language. It must not invent belief, identity, Mission, values, relationships, motives, outcomes, or commitments.

<a id="T4-B0167"></a>
Before a meaningful change, show the proposed wording, its intended location and scope, and—when replacing approved content—the current wording and reason for revision. Recognize clear approval without repeatedly seeking it. Silence, continued conversation, an assessment score, or acceptance of help is not approval of a particular statement.

<a id="T4-B0168"></a>
Preserve exact Participant Approved wording. A new proposed version does not displace it. Keep the previous wording and approval history when an approved revision becomes current. Relocating unchanged content within v4 is not permission to change its meaning, scope, or permissions.

<a id="T4-B0169"></a>
A short North Star summary, cover anchor, or public-facing excerpt requires accurate selection and appropriate approval. Approval of longer wording does not authorize a materially different condensed statement.

<a id="T4-B0170"></a>
Respect corrections, uncertainty, private/offline work, declined disclosure, pauses, and later review. The participant can approve a limited seasonal statement without claiming permanent certainty. Approval of intended practice is not evidence it happened; later outcomes must be recorded separately.

<a id="T4-B0171"></a>
Journals, assessments, activity data, sensitive disclosures, and interpretations do not automatically become defining Blueprint statements. Use only authorized material for a legitimate Blueprint purpose. Keep framework convictions, participant beliefs, and suggested application distinct; ordinary help does not depend on manufacturing doctrinal agreement.

<a id="T4-B0172"></a>
Approval of wording is separate from permission to share or perform an external action. The Blueprint’s approval/version information must not imply an integration, storage action, or human agreement that has not actually been confirmed.

<a id="T4-B0173"></a>
### 11. Human-accountability/community rules

<a id="T4-B0174"></a>
The Blueprint should strengthen church participation, marriage where applicable, family, friendships, small groups, mentorship, accountability, pastoral support, and appropriate professional support. Human relationships are part of the life being stewarded, not merely mechanisms for completing tasks.

<a id="T4-B0175"></a>
Do not design the document to increase dependence on Steward or portray Steward as the participant’s community, pastor, confidant, or human accountability partner. It supports preparation, reflection, and follow-through around real relationships; it does not replace them.

<a id="T4-B0176"></a>
The participant’s approved support structure may include one person, several people with different roles, or a group. Clarify who is invited, who has agreed, what each role supports, the proposed or agreed cadence, the selected questions, and what information may be discussed. Do not grant a spouse, pastor, group leader, mentor, or other person automatic access because of their role.

<a id="T4-B0177"></a>
For a married participant, apply the current Sensitive Conversations standard: do not recommend an ongoing private, emotionally intimate personal confidant or accountability relationship with an opposite-sex person other than the spouse. Normally use a safe spouse relationship, trusted same-sex friend/accountability partner or mentor, appropriate same-sex group relationship, or properly bounded professional support. Qualified opposite-sex professional or pastoral care is not automatically prohibited. Immediate safety overrides the ordinary relational-integrity preference. This incorporates the existing standard without expanding it.

<a id="T4-B0178"></a>
Keep trusted community/accountability, pastoral/spiritual care, and licensed or specialized professional care distinguishable. They may complement one another; none should be assigned responsibilities beyond its role. Seeking appropriate qualified help can be wise stewardship. This template does not provide diagnosis, treatment, or a clinical protocol.

<a id="T4-B0179"></a>
Accountability should invite honesty about previous commitments, actual experience, relevant drift, and the participant’s chosen next response. It must not become surveillance, management of another adult’s life, control over family members, or a demand for intimate disclosures as proof of faithfulness.

<a id="T4-B0180"></a>
Sharing requires selected content, named recipients, and explicit consent. An invitation to involve someone is not proof of consent, contact, acceptance, or disclosure. Respect a participant who is not ready to involve someone, and never direct disclosure or reconnection toward an unsafe person merely to satisfy the template.

<a id="T4-B0181"></a>
### 12. Legacy standard

<a id="T4-B0182"></a>
The exact definitions in 04_SB_Legacy_and_Legacy_Giving_Standard_v1 remain governing:

<a id="T4-B0183"></a>
Legacy: “Legacy is what your life multiplies in people and through them while you are still living—not merely what is remembered or transferred after you die.”

<a id="T4-B0184"></a>
Legacy Giving: “Legacy Giving is multiplying your impact while you are still living by modeling, teaching and helping the next generation and others practice a lifestyle of generosity and pass it on.”

<a id="T4-B0185"></a>
Apply these definitions consistently across the North Star, Finances, Guardrails, rhythms, Free starting plan, and Legacy page. In participant application, Legacy Giving means modeling, teaching, and equipping willing people to practice and pass on generosity while the giver is living. Estate continuity supports that generous life; it does not define the practice.

<a id="T4-B0186"></a>
Model + Explain makes the purpose behind generosity visible while protecting appropriate privacy. Practice Together invites a willing person into an understandable shared practice. Entrust + Pass On offers a bounded next step the other person may choose or lead and encourages equipping someone else. Reflect considers what was learned and what the person may choose next.

<a id="T4-B0187"></a>
These are adaptable practice prompts, not four new named phases, a compulsory sequence, or recurring obligations for every participant. Parenthood, age, retirement, wealth, large gifts, and public influence are not prerequisites. Do not require a donation, recruit, particular organization, or another person’s compliance to claim a useful beginning.

<a id="T4-B0188"></a>
Distinguish a gift meeting a need from the additional work of helping another person learn and practice generosity. Both may occur together. An estate plan may support continuity, but documents, beneficiaries, trusts, and charitable bequests do not replace living formation.

<a id="T4-B0189"></a>
Do not claim that another person has learned, changed, agreed, or passed generosity onward merely because an invitation or plan exists. Keep intentions, reported practices, and reported outcomes separate. Legacy is not a completion score, and the participant cannot guarantee another person’s choices or make the scale of impact the measure of personal worth.

<a id="T4-B0190"></a>
Retain earlier estate reflections in their historical form. Propose living-practice additions separately and obtain participant approval; do not silently rewrite earlier approved content when applying the current standard.

### 13. Export/formatting principles

<a id="T4-B0192"></a>
Voice and selection. Use short first-person participant-facing statements that sound recognizably like the participant. Avoid generic AI language, borrowed identity labels, and inspirational padding. Use names and personal details only when supplied, appropriate, and approved. General template language remains gender-neutral unless an explicitly tailored edition applies.

<a id="T4-B0193"></a>
Readable visual system. Retain the established cream, navy, gold, teal, and green system where useful, with strong contrast. Template v3’s body-text guidance is generally 11–12 points, with 13–15 points for key statements. Use clear hierarchy, adequate spacing, and readable labels. Do not shrink text or pack boxes merely to retain unnecessary prose. Meaning must not depend on color alone.

<a id="T4-B0194"></a>
Page discipline. Full has thirteen pages in the specified order; Free has three. Use one architecture-style North Star only. Avoid blank decorative pages, repeated framework diagrams, and repetitive box layouts that make unlike content look identical. Keep headings, pagination, and section labels consistent. An unfinished page should honestly display its useful content and state rather than invented filler.

<a id="T4-B0195"></a>
Depth without overcrowding. Longer reflections, detailed stories, assessment responses, activity logs, financial workings, supporting evidence, and technical notes remain in their appropriate authorized profile/journal/supporting records. They do not automatically enter the exported Blueprint. When approved content is too long, obtain approval for a concise rendering or select a meaning-preserving approved excerpt; never silently rewrite it.

<a id="T4-B0196"></a>
Status integrity. Include the current season, actual revision/version information, and clear indications where content is Proposed, Taking Shape, Not Yet Explored, or genuinely Needs Clarity. A visually polished page is not proof that its content is approved. Avoid completion percentages, spiritual grades, and claims that Faith, Family, or Legacy is finished. Distinguish a proposed draft from a participant-approved current version.

<a id="T4-B0197"></a>
Approval and privacy. Select the intended export content and audience deliberately. Generating a document does not automatically share it, make it public, or authorize access to supporting records. Preserve exact approved wording and the separate status of proposed changes. No real participant content is required for prototype design testing.

<a id="T4-B0198"></a>
Scripture and rights. Until production Scripture rights and permissions are approved, prototypes use Scripture references only. Production exports must follow the then-current approved Scripture and rights standard. Full translation text in older examples does not itself authorize production use. Do not alter an approved underlying statement to accommodate an export restriction—use an authorized presentation or request review. Theological/content authority and publication/licensing rights remain separate.

<a id="T4-B0199"></a>
Final quality check. Confirm that the document identifies what matters, distinguishes enduring direction from seasonal focus, keeps all Four Pillars visible, shows what to protect and how to return, includes sustainable practice and renewal, supports real people, and represents Legacy as living multiplication. Check that an ordinary difficult decision becomes easier without implying a perfect life, requiring every field to be resolved, or turning every healthy area into a project.

<a id="LG-B0004"></a>
Applies to the framework, books, Academy lessons, challenges, assessments, Personal Blueprints, website messaging and Steward guidance. This standard records Mike’s clarification; it is not a fourth wealth phase or another course to purchase.

<a id="LG-B0005"></a>
#### Legacy

<a id="LG-B0006"></a>
Legacy is what your life multiplies in people and through them while you are still living—not merely what is remembered or transferred after you die.

<a id="LG-B0007"></a>
#### Legacy Giving

<a id="LG-B0008"></a>
Legacy Giving is multiplying your impact while you are still living by modeling, teaching and helping the next generation and others practice a lifestyle of generosity and pass it on.

<a id="LG-B0009"></a>
#### The supporting role of estate planning

<a id="LG-B0010"></a>
Good estate documents and a plan for generosity after death remain important. They support the continuity of a generous life; they do not replace forming generous people now.

<a id="LG-B0011"></a>
#### The Three Phases of Giving remain connected

<a id="LG-B0012"></a>
Tithing: faithful, planned giving through the local church, retaining the source teaching’s ten-percent definition. Impact Giving: purposeful giving beyond the ongoing tithe to people, ministries and causes. Legacy Giving: helping others learn to live generously and pass it on while you are still living.

<a id="LG-B0013"></a>
The pairing remains Foundation/Tithing, Accumulating Wealth/Impact Giving, and Strategic Income/Legacy Giving. The practices can overlap in any financial season. They are not spiritual ranks, age thresholds, wealth requirements or prerequisites for loving and teaching others.

<a id="LG-B0014"></a>
#### A gift can meet a need. A life of generosity can help form another generous person. Legacy Giving intentionally includes that multiplication.

<a id="LG-B0015"></a>
PIVOTAL TEACHING  /  SCRIPTURE & MEANING

<a id="LG-B0016"></a>
### Do not only give. Help generosity grow in others.

<a id="LG-B0017"></a>
2 Timothy 2:2 (NIV)

<a id="LG-B0018"></a>
And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others.

<a id="LG-B0019"></a>
Paul’s instruction concerns entrusting teaching to people able to teach others. The lesson draws on that pattern of equipping and passing on; it does not turn the verse into a promise of financial returns, a multiplication formula or a quota for influence.

<a id="LG-B0020"></a>
2 Corinthians 9:7 (NIV)

<a id="LG-B0021"></a>
Each of you should give what you have decided in your heart to give, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.

<a id="LG-B0022"></a>
Generosity is not coerced. The people learning alongside you retain their own choices; a shared practice is an invitation, not a loyalty test.

<a id="LG-B0023"></a>
#### The difference is formation, not merely timing

<a id="LG-B0024"></a>
Funding a food ministry is Impact Giving. Explaining why it matters, serving alongside a willing learner, letting them plan an appropriate next opportunity and encouraging them to help someone else learn makes it an expression of Legacy Giving too. The gift and the formation can happen together. A future bequest can sustain the ministry, but the paperwork does not replace this present work.

<a id="LG-B0025"></a>
#### What makes the lesson pivotal

<a id="LG-B0026"></a>
The question changes from “What will people receive when I am gone?” to “Who is learning to live generously through me now?” A young adult can practice this with a friend; a parent with a child; a leader with a mentee; and a retiree with any willing person in their community. Parenthood, a large gift and retirement are not requirements.

<a id="LG-B0027"></a>
#### My responsibility is to model, teach, invite and equip faithfully. I cannot guarantee another person’s choices or make impact the measure of my worth.

<a id="LG-B0028"></a>
PRACTICE  /  ADAPTABLE TO ANY SEASON

<a id="LG-B0029"></a>
### A living Legacy Giving practice

<a id="LG-B0030"></a>
The following sequence makes the lesson practical. It is a teaching aid, not a new named phase system. Choose one small experiment; do not turn every step into a permanent recurring obligation.

<a id="LG-B0031"></a>
#### Model and explain

<a id="LG-B0032"></a>
Let someone encounter a real example of generosity. Explain the purpose, the people it serves and the thought behind it. Share only what is appropriate; private giving amounts and another person’s story need not be disclosed.

<a id="LG-B0033"></a>
#### Practice together

<a id="LG-B0034"></a>
Invite a willing person to help evaluate a cause, serve, plan a gift or notice a need. Ask what they think and what matters to them. Fit the practice to age, capacity and the current season.

<a id="LG-B0035"></a>
#### Entrust and encourage passing it on

<a id="LG-B0036"></a>
Give them an appropriate next step they can choose or lead. Later, ask what they learned and whether they would like to help someone else learn. A service-planning role does not create legal authority over money. No person, gift or invitation is required for Academy credit.

<a id="LG-B0048"></a>

#### Welcome from Michael

<a id="CFM4-B0016"></a>
Most people do not drift because they do not care. They drift because life gets full. Responsibilities grow. Calendars fill. Work expands. Family needs attention. Financial pressure builds. Health gets pushed aside. Joy becomes something people hope to get back to someday. And somewhere along the way, many people stop asking one of the most important questions they could ever ask:

<a id="CFM4-B0017"></a>
What kind of life am I actually building?

<a id="CFM4-B0018"></a>
The Created for More Check-In is not designed to make you feel guilty or overwhelmed. It is not a test you pass or fail. It is a mirror and a map—a way to pause, look honestly at the direction of your life, and see where you are standing on solid ground and where you may need greater attention.

<a id="CFM4-B0019"></a>

WELCOME

<a id="EA-B0003"></a>
Welcome from Michael

<a id="EA-B0004"></a>
Most people do not run out of purpose all at once. They run out of energy first.

<a id="EA-B0005"></a>
They still love their family. They still care about their work. They still believe in their calling. They still want to make wise decisions, build meaningful relationships, honor God, and lead well. But they are tired — not only physically, but emotionally worn down, mentally scattered, and spiritually dry.

<a id="EA-B0006"></a>
The Energy Audit is a companion to the Created for More Check-In. Where that check-in gives you a wide-angle view of your whole life, this audit zooms in on one specific layer of the Stewardship Blueprint: the energy that makes everything else possible.

<a id="EA-B0007"></a>
You can have a clear purpose, a strong marriage, disciplined finances, and good intentions, and still find the whole structure straining if your energy is depleted. Energy is not a side issue in a well-built life. It is the current that makes everything else livable.

<a id="EA-B0008"></a>
I approach this work from a Christian faith perspective, because that is the lens through which I have learned to understand rest, purpose, and renewal. But this tool is faith-based, not faith-exclusive. Wherever you are starting from, you are welcome here. The invitation is simple: slow down, tell yourself the truth, and start paying attention to what is fueling — or draining — the life you are building.

<a id="EA-B0009"></a>
WHY IT MATTERS

<a id="EA-B0010"></a>
Why This Audit Matters

<a id="EA-B0011"></a>
Busyness Can Disguise Depletion

<a id="EA-B0012"></a>
Most people are not running on empty because they are lazy. They are running on empty because they keep saying yes, keep pushing, and keep telling themselves they will rest later. The danger is rarely a lack of effort. It is effort without renewal.

<a id="EA-B0013"></a>
Energy Debt Is Real

<a id="EA-B0014"></a>
Energy is entrusted capital, and like any account, it runs on deposits and withdrawals. When demand exceeds capacity, we start making shortcut withdrawals — skip the sleep, skip the workout, skip the quiet moment, skip the honest conversation. Those withdrawals cover the moment, but no deposit ever follows. Over time, the account runs into overdraft, and the debt comes due — usually in the form of a short temper, a foggy mind, a distant marriage, or a dry spiritual life.

<a id="EA-B0015"></a>
You Cannot Steward What You Never Renew

<a id="EA-B0016"></a>
Time and money get most of the attention in stewardship conversations. But energy is the resource beneath both. You can have time on the calendar and money in the bank and still lack the capacity to use either one well.

<a id="EA-B0017"></a>
This audit will help you evaluate four sources of energy: Physical, Emotional, Mental, and Spiritual.

<a id="EA-B0018"></a>
These connect to the sixth layer of the larger Stewardship Blueprint framework — Energy — which sits alongside Faith, Purpose, the Four Pillars, Guardrails, Keystone Habits, and Legacy.

<a id="EA-B0019"></a>
A SHORT TEACHING

<a id="EA-B0020"></a>
Understanding Your Four Energies

<a id="EA-B0021"></a>
Two Patterns

<a id="EA-B0022"></a>
Every tank runs on the same principle, whether it holds gasoline or personal energy: what you draw out has to be put back in, or eventually you run dry. Most people default to one of two patterns.

<a id="EA-B0023"></a>
—  Running on Empty is chronic output with no refueling — pushing continuously, never stopping to fill back up, until the tank hits zero. Fatigue quietly turns into errors, short tempers, and eventually burnout.

<a id="EA-B0024"></a>
—  The Refueling Cycle is the healthier pattern — seasons of full output followed by real refueling, so capacity is renewed instead of slowly drained.

<a id="EA-B0025"></a>
Life itself was designed around refueling: day and night, work and rest, planting and harvest. Sabbath is not a suggestion. It is architecture. If Jesus practiced rhythms of withdrawal and refueling in the middle of the most important mission in history, it is worth asking why we assume we can run on empty indefinitely.

<a id="EA-B0026"></a>
This idea of managing your tank rather than just your hours builds on research by Jim Loehr and Tony Schwartz, whose work on energy and performance first framed capacity as something trained through cycles of stress and recovery. Running on Empty and the Refueling Cycle are this book's own language for that same truth, shaped for the Stewardship Blueprint.

<a id="EA-B0027"></a>
The Four Energies

<a id="EA-B0028"></a>
—  Physical Energy gives you the capacity to act, endure, and stay consistent. It is shaped by sleep, movement, nutrition, and recovery.

<a id="EA-B0029"></a>
—  Emotional Energy shapes the tone you bring into your marriage, your parenting, your leadership, and your friendships. It is the difference between responding and reacting.

<a id="EA-B0030"></a>
—  Mental Energy determines your focus, your clarity, and your ability to make wise decisions instead of scattered ones. It is shaped by what you allow into your attention.

<a id="EA-B0031"></a>
—  Spiritual Energy is the Source beneath the other three — your connection to God, to purpose, and to something bigger than your own effort. When this one runs dry, everything else eventually does too.

<a id="EA-B0032"></a>
None of these operate in isolation. A depleted body affects your emotional tone. A scattered mind affects your spiritual clarity. A dry spiritual life affects your motivation to move your body at all. Stewarding energy means paying attention to all four — not just the one that is loudest right now.

<a id="EA-B0083"></a>
DIRECTION, NOT JUST DIAGNOSIS

<a id="EA-B0084"></a>
What Refills the Tank

<a id="EA-B0085"></a>
A score tells you where you are. It doesn't tell you what to do next. This audit is meant to do both — to wake you up to where your tank actually sits, and to point you toward what genuinely refills it. Below are proven starting points for each energy source. You don't need all of them. You need one.

<a id="EA-B0086"></a>
Refilling Physical Energy

<a id="EA-B0087"></a>
—  Protect a consistent sleep window — the regularity matters as much as the total hours.

<a id="EA-B0088"></a>
—  Move your body daily, even briefly. A walk counts as a deposit.

<a id="EA-B0089"></a>
—  Build one real day of rest into your week — not just a lighter day, a different day.

<a id="EA-B0090"></a>
—  Fuel with food that sustains energy, not just food that's convenient in the moment.

<a id="EA-B0091"></a>
Refilling Emotional Energy

<a id="EA-B0092"></a>
—  Name what you're actually feeling before you react to it.

<a id="EA-B0093"></a>
—  Keep at least one relationship where you're fully honest, not just informed.

<a id="EA-B0094"></a>
—  Practice gratitude on purpose — don't wait until you feel like it.

<a id="EA-B0095"></a>
—  Make room for laughter, play, and lightness, not only responsibility.

<a id="EA-B0096"></a>
Refilling Mental Energy

<a id="EA-B0097"></a>
—  Protect blocks of undistracted, single-focus time.

<a id="EA-B0098"></a>
—  Take real breaks from screens — not just a different screen.

<a id="EA-B0099"></a>
—  Simplify what you can. Fewer daily decisions leaves more capacity for the ones that matter.

<a id="EA-B0100"></a>
—  Get unhurried, quiet thinking time — a walk, a drive, a journal, a porch.

<a id="EA-B0101"></a>
Refilling Spiritual Energy

<a id="EA-B0102"></a>
—  Return to a consistent rhythm of prayer or Scripture, even a short one.

<a id="EA-B0103"></a>
—  Practice Sabbath as a full stop, not just a slower pace.

<a id="EA-B0104"></a>
—  Serve someone else. Purpose renews capacity as much as rest does.

<a id="EA-B0105"></a>
—  Surrender what you can't control instead of carrying it alone.

<a id="EA-B0106"></a>
None of this requires a life overhaul. Refilling the tank is rarely one big change — it's a small, repeated rhythm that eventually becomes a Keystone Habit. Look at your lowest score from the previous pages, pick one item from that list, and start there.

FOR FACILITATORS & SPEAKERS

<a id="EA-B0135"></a>
Using This With a Group

<a id="EA-B0136"></a>
This assessment is built first as a self-guided experience — something you can work through on your own, online, in fifteen quiet minutes. But if you ever use it in a live setting — a small group, a men's study, a classroom, or a speaking engagement — here is a simple way to lead it well.

<a id="EA-B0137"></a>
Suggested Format (30–40 minutes)

<a id="EA-B0138"></a>
—  Teaching (10 min) — Walk through “Understanding Your Four Energies” and the Running on Empty vs. Refueling Cycle pattern.

<a id="EA-B0139"></a>
—  Individual Reflection (10–15 min) — Give the group quiet time to complete the audit privately.

<a id="EA-B0140"></a>
—  Group Discussion (10–15 min) — Invite, but do not require, volunteers to share their lowest energy source and one next step. Two or three of the reflection questions work well here.

<a id="EA-B0141"></a>
—  Close (2–3 min) — Invite one commitment per person — not a full plan, just one honest next step.

<a id="EA-B0142"></a>
Facilitator Notes

<a id="EA-B0143"></a>
—  Do not rush the individual reflection time. The value is in the honesty, not the speed.

<a id="EA-B0144"></a>
—  Normalize a low score. Most rooms will have more “Running on Reserves” than “Fully Engaged.” Naming that up front lowers defensiveness.
